The 10-step roadmap

A realistic route from the first letter to independent conversation. Timings assume 30–45 minutes a day; slower is fine. Each step ends with a checkpoint — move on when it is met, not when the chapter feels finished.

Four rules that matter more than the schedule

  • Daily beats weekly. Twenty minutes every day outperforms three hours on Sunday.
  • Read aloud from day one. Silent reading hides stress and vowel reduction; the voice is the skill.
  • One grammar point per week. Apply it in ten sentences before adding the next.
  • Review yesterday before today. Ten minutes on old words is part of the new lesson.

The path

  1. 01

    Alphabet and sounds

    Weeks 1–2 · 30 min/day

    Decode any word and read it aloud with the right stress.

    • 33 letters, their names, and the hard/soft pairs: 10 vowels, 21 consonants, 2 silent signs.
    • Vowel reduction: unstressed о becomes [ɐ] or [ə]; final devoicing turns друг into [druk].
    • Read syllables and short words aloud daily. Grammar can wait two weeks.
    • Learn ten words a day from the dictionary, always with the stress mark.

    Checkpoint: read 20 random dictionary headwords aloud and compare with the recordings.

  2. 02

    First phrases and questions

    Week 2 · 30 min/day

    Survive a first exchange without switching to English.

    • Greetings, introductions, thanks, apologies: learn whole phrases, not isolated words.
    • Question words: кто, что, где, куда, откуда, почему, зачем, как, сколько.
    • Numbers 1–20, days of the week, and the polite вы-form.
    • Shadow dialogue 1 line by line; record yourself and compare with the audio.

    Checkpoint: an eight-sentence self-introduction from memory and five questions asked aloud.

  3. 03

    Gender, number, nominative

    Weeks 2–3 · 30 min/day

    Build correct noun phrases and simple descriptions.

    • Gender from the ending; ь-nouns must be memorised: день is masculine, ночь is feminine.
    • Plural formation, including the fleeting vowel (окно → окна, genitive окон).
    • Adjective agreement: новый файл, новая книга, новое окно, новые файлы.
    • No articles and no present-tense copula: «Это файл», not «Это есть файл».

    Checkpoint: describe 15 objects with adjective + noun and give 10 plural forms.

  4. 04

    Present tense and conjugation

    Weeks 3–4 · 30 min/day

    Talk about routine, facts, and preferences.

    • Two conjugations: читаешь / говоришь; stem changes such as писать → пишу.
    • Reflexive -ся: заниматься, нравиться, учиться.
    • Negation with не; double negation is obligatory with negative pronouns.
    • Time markers: сейчас, обычно, каждый день, иногда, никогда.

    Checkpoint: conjugate 20 verbs in writing and produce 10 sentences about a daily routine.

    VerbsDictionary

  5. 05

    Accusative and genitive

    Weeks 4–6 · 35 min/day

    Handle objects, absence, possession, and quantity.

    • Accusative for direct objects; animacy copies the genitive (вижу кота, but вижу стол).
    • Genitive after нет, без, из, от, до, у; possession and quantity.
    • Genitive plural is the hardest form: книг, мест, файлов, братьев.
    • Learn each new noun with its genitive plural from the dictionary.

    Checkpoint: 30 sentences contrasting «вижу X» and «нет X», plus 10 quantity phrases.

  6. 06

    Dative, instrumental, prepositional

    Weeks 6–8 · 35 min/day

    Talk about people, tools, places, and topics.

    • Dative: recipient and experiencer — дать мне, мне нужно, ему нравится.
    • Instrumental: с + person, instrument (ручкой), profession (стал инженером).
    • Prepositional: location and topic, always with a preposition — в файле, о работе.
    • Learn prepositions as case triggers, not as one-to-one translations.

    Checkpoint: describe a commute, a tool, and a conversation topic in 12 sentences.

  7. 07

    Aspect, past, future

    Weeks 8–10 · 40 min/day

    Choose between process and completed result without hesitating.

    • Every verb belongs to an aspect pair; perfective marks one completed result.
    • Past tense agrees with gender and number, never with person: я делал / я делала.
    • Future: буду + imperfective infinitive, or the perfective present form.
    • Build a personal list of 30 pairs: делать/сделать, писать/написать, проверять/проверить.

    Checkpoint: retell yesterday in 12 sentences and justify each aspect choice.

  8. 08

    Motion verbs and modality

    Weeks 10–12 · 40 min/day

    Describe routes, habits, plans, permission, and necessity.

    • Unidirectional vs multidirectional: идти/ходить, ехать/ездить, лететь/летать.
    • Prefixes that change direction: при-, у-, по-, вы-, до-.
    • Impersonal modality: нужно, надо, можно, нельзя + infinitive, with a dative person.
    • Distinguish location (где?) from direction (куда?) in every sentence.

    Checkpoint: 15 sentences about routes and plans, plus spoken directions to a landmark.

  9. 09

    Complex sentences and word order

    Months 3–4 · 40 min/day

    Connect ideas and control emphasis.

    • Subordinators: что, чтобы, если, хотя, пока, потому что; a comma precedes each clause.
    • Neutral subject–verb–object versus marked order: given information first, new information last.
    • Particles that carry stance: же, ведь, -то, вот, ну, уж.

The language in one page

Russian is not “English with different words”. Almost every design decision is different.

Russian (ру́сский язы́к, rússkiy yazýk) is an East Slavic language in the Indo-European family, written in the Cyrillic alphabet. It has roughly 150 million native speakers and another 100 million who use it as a second language. It is the official language of the Russian Federation and a state or official language in Belarus, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, and it remains widely used across the former Soviet Union.

Typologically it is fusional and synthetic: meaning is carried by endings fused into single morphemes rather than by separate words. Where English says of the book, Russian says кни́ги — one ending, three grammatical facts (feminine, singular, genitive). Russian has no articles, no fixed word order, no present-tense copula, and a verbal system organised around aspect rather than around tense.

The headline numbers: 33 letters, 6 cases, 3 genders plus animacy, 2 numbers, 2 aspects, 3 tenses in practice, and a stress accent that is free, mobile, and not written.

Sound system

Russian has five vowel phonemes — /a e i o u/ — plus /ɨ/, written ы. The crucial fact for a learner is vowel reduction: unstressed о is pronounced [ɐ] or [ə], so молоко́ sounds like məlɐˈko, not moloko. Unstressed е tends toward [ɪ], which is why язы́к is yizýk in running speech.

Consonants come in hard/soft pairs: т vs ть (palatalised), л vs ль. Softness is phonemic — угол “corner” and уголь “coal” differ only in that one feature. Voiced consonants devoice word-finally and before voiceless ones: друг is druk; второ́й is ftaróy. ш ж ц are always hard; ч щ й are always soft.

Stress is lexical, free, and mobile: it can fall on any syllable, and it moves between forms of the same word (го́род “city”, города́ “cities”, в го́роде “in the city”). Ё is always stressed; Russians routinely write е instead, which is why this page marks stress explicitly.

What Russian leaves out

  • Articles. No a/an/the. Definiteness is inferred from word order, case, and context.
  • The verb “to be” in the present. Это файл. — “This is a file.” No verb.
  • Subject pronouns. The ending already encodes person, so отвеча́ю is “I answer” without я.
  • “Have”. Russian says “at me there is”: у меня́ есть вопро́с — literally “by me there-is a question”.

Grammar, in enough detail to be useful

Cases first — they are the part that makes Russian look like code.

The six cases

A case ending marks the role a noun plays. English does this with word order and prepositions; Russian does it with the noun itself. Кни́га (nominative) is the subject; кни́гу (accusative) is the object; кни́ги (genitive) is “of the book”; кни́ге (dative/prepositional) is “to the book” or “about the book”.

Two wrinkles worth knowing. First, animacy: for masculine singular and all plurals, the accusative of a living thing copies the genitive — ви́жу кота́ (like кота́) but ви́жу стол (like the nominative). Second, a handful of nouns have a second locative used only with в and на: в лесу́ “in the forest”, на мосту́ “on the bridge”.

Nouns: gender, number, declension

Gender is predictable from the nominative ending most of the time: masculine typically ends in a consonant (стол) or -й/-ь; feminine in -а/-я or -ь; neuter in -о/-е. The -ь nouns are the trap: день is masculine, ночь is feminine. Learn the gender with the word.

There are three productive declension patterns. Hard-stem masculine/neuter (стол, окно́), soft-stem masculine/neuter and feminine (день, по́ле, неде́ля), and the small -ь feminine class (ночь, мышь) which looks like the soft pattern but has its own endings. Irregulars are few but frequent: челове́к → лю́ди, год → го́ды/лет, вре́мя (neuter in -мя), путь.

Plural is not just “add -ы”. The genitive plural is the notorious one: кни́га → книг (zero ending), ме́сто → мест, стол → столо́в, брат → бра́тьев. A zero ending after a hard consonant often inserts a fleeting vowel: окно́ → о́кон.

Adjectives

Adjectives agree with the noun in gender, number and case, and the ending carries all of it: но́вый файл, но́вая кни́га, но́вое окно́, но́вые фа́йлы. In oblique cases the masculine and neuter merge (но́вого фа́йла).

There are also short forms, used predicatively: Файл гото́в. “The file is ready.” And comparison is synthetic: бы́стрый → быстре́е, with са́мый for the superlative (са́мый бы́стрый), plus a few suppletive forms (хоро́ший → лу́чше).

Verbs: aspect is the engine

Every Russian verb belongs to an aspect pair. The imperfective describes a process, a habit, or a repeated action; the perfective describes a single completed result. Both members translate to the same English verb, which is why dictionaries list them together: де́лать / сде́лать “to do”, писа́ть / написа́ть “to write”, проверя́ть / провери́ть “to check”.

Tense is then mostly a matter of aspect. Imperfective has three forms — past, present, future (де́лал, де́лаю, бу́ду де́лать); perfective has only two — past and future (сде́лал, сде́лаю), because a completed action cannot be in progress now. The perfective “present” form is the future.

Past tense does not agree with person at all; it agrees with gender and number: я де́лал / я де́лала, мы де́лали. The ending set is -л, -ла, -ло, -ли. Present/future endings fall into two conjugations (-ешь/-ет vs -ишь/-ит), and the stem often changes: писа́ть → пишу́, пи́шешь.

Two more features do a lot of work. Reflexive verbs take -ся/-сь and often correspond to English intransitives or passives: открыва́ться “to open (by itself)”. Verbs of motion come in directional/non-directional pairs — идти́/ходи́ть “go on foot”, е́хать/е́здить “go by vehicle” — and the choice is grammatical, not stylistic.

Modality is often impersonal: ну́жно “it is necessary”, мо́жно “it is possible / one may”, нельзя́ “it is forbidden / impossible”, на́до “one must”. They take an infinitive and no subject: Ну́жно прове́рить. “It needs checking.”

Pronouns and numerals

Personal pronouns decline, and the third person adds an н- after a preposition: у него́, к ней. Свой is a reflexive possessive that refers back to the subject — a word English simply lacks.

Numerals govern the genitive. Оди́н agrees like an adjective; два, три, четы́ре take genitive singular (два фа́йла); пять and above take genitive plural (пять фа́йлов); and numbers ending in 1 take the nominative singular again (два́дцать оди́н файл). This is why “21 files” and “22 files” have different noun forms.

Word order and syntax

Russian word order is free but not arbitrary. The neutral order is Subject–Verb–Object, but the order encodes information structure: what is given comes first, what is new comes last. Ма́ша чита́ет кни́гу answers “what is Masha doing?”; Кни́гу чита́ет Ма́ша answers “who is reading the book?”. Same words, different focus. Intonation does the rest.

The present-tense copula is omitted, and a dash stands in for it when both sides are nouns: Москва́ — столи́ца. “Moscow is the capital.” Negation is не before the verb, and it is obligatory with negative pronouns — double negation is the norm: Никто́ не пришёл. literally “Nobody didn’t come.”

Questions usually need no inversion, only a question word or a rising contour: Ты гото́в? “Are you ready?” Subordination uses что “that”, что́бы “in order that”, е́сли “if”, хотя́ “although”, пока́ “while/until”.

Pragmatics: how Russians actually talk

Ты vs вы. Вы is polite or plural; ты is intimate or informal. The choice is a social act, not a grammar error, and switching without invitation is noticeable. Verb endings follow: вы чита́ете, ты чита́ешь.

Particles. Russian conversation runs on small untranslatable words: же (emphasis, “after all”), ведь (shared knowledge, “you know”), -то (topic marker), вот (presentational “here”), ну (hedging, “well”), уж (resignation, “already/really”). They carry the stance that English puts in intonation.

Diminutives are not only “small”: файлик can be affectionate or dismissive, зада́чка can mean “a little task” or “a nasty problem”. Politeness also lives in the imperative plus пожа́луйста, and in the conditional-softened не могли́ бы вы… “could you possibly…”.

Typography

Russian uses «guillemets» for quotation, an em dash (—) for the omitted copula and for appositions, and a comma before что, что́бы, е́сли, когда́ in subordinate clauses. Decimal fractions take a comma (3,14), thousands a space (1 000 000). Ё is legally optional in print, which is why learners meet ещё and еще in the same text.
